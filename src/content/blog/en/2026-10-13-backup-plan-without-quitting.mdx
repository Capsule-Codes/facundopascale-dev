I had been fired once before, suddenly. One day I had a job and the next I didn't. Between that job and the next one I freelanced to survive, without knowing how much would come in the following month. It wasn't a romantic chapter of "independence". It was checking my bank account more often than I'd like to admit.

When I got the next job, the first thing I did was start an agency with Miguel, my partner: [Capsule Codes](https://www.capsulecodes.com/#products). Not because I wanted to be an entrepreneur. Because I didn't want to be back at zero.

This post is about that: building a backup plan while you have a job, what went well, what didn't, and what I'd do differently if I started today.

## Story: a backup plan built at night

[FACU: la semana en que fundaron la agencia: cómo lo hablaron con Miguel, qué fue lo primero que hicieron (nombre, papeles, primer cliente), dónde estabas]

The agency billed from the first month, and not by magic. It billed because I brought the freelance clients I already had. That's the first idea I want to make clear: **a backup plan isn't built from nothing; it's built on top of something you're already doing.** I didn't start with a brilliant idea and a logo. I started with real work that already existed and put a structure around it.

For more than a year it was nights and weekends. During the day I had another job, with its hours and its responsibilities. The agency lived in the gaps.

[FACU: cómo era una semana típica en esa época: horarios, qué sacrificabas, cómo lo manejabas con tu vida personal]

Then my contract ended. And that's where I found the uncomfortable part: I needed the backup plan before it was ready. It existed, it had clients, it had products underway, but it wasn't designed to carry me overnight. A backup plan that complements a salary is one thing. A backup plan that has to replace it is a very different one.

## Proof: what Capsule Codes is today

Today Capsule Codes builds mobile, web and desktop apps end to end: from the idea and the design to the deploy and the maintenance. The split is clear:

- **Miguel**, from Italy, runs sales and negotiation.
- **I**, from Argentina, run development.
- We hire in Argentina, and we find clients remotely.

As an agency we have three apps published in the stores. We also build our own products:

- **[Stagionaly](/en/work/stagionaly/)**: it came out of the years Miguel worked in hotels in Italy. He knows from the inside how seasonal hiring works, and the product comes from that experience: a job board for Italian seasonal hospitality.
- **[Orbys](/en/work/orbys/)**: the tool we use to run the agency: hours, projects, expenses, billing.
- **[Elevate](/en/work/elevate/)**: a suite for trainers, nutritionists and gyms. The website is live, the mobile apps aren't in the stores yet, and the desktop version is in pre-release.

## Lesson: four things I'd do differently (or the same, but sooner)

### 1. Start before you need it

The best time to build a backup plan is when you don't need one. Plans built out of fear go badly: you take any client, you charge less than your work is worth, you decide with end-of-month urgency instead of judgment. I started while I had a stable job, and even so I didn't have enough time for it to be ready when I needed it. If I had waited until I was out of a job to begin, I would have started from the exact place I wanted to escape. Starting with a salary gives you something you can't buy later: the ability to say no.

### 2. Don't make the backup plan "find another job"

"If they fire me, I'll find another one" isn't a backup plan. It's plan A again, with the same dependency: someone else decides whether you have an income. A real backup plan is something that exists and works in parallel, even if it's small. In my case it was freelance clients turned into an agency. It can be something else: a product, a service, consulting. What matters is that it doesn't depend on a single company choosing you.

### 3. Find a partner who knows what you don't

I know how to build software. What I don't know, or didn't, is how to sell, negotiate and sustain a commercial relationship. Miguel does. If I had started the agency alone, I would have had a development shop with no new clients. A partner isn't someone who thinks like you; it's someone who covers what you lack. And there's a less obvious benefit: when it's late and you're tired, having another person pushing changes everything.

### 4. Don't wait for the product to be finished to start selling

This is the one that cost us the most, and I'm still learning it. We spent a year building three products, and only now are we learning to sell them. Building is comfortable for a developer: there's a technical problem, you solve it, it feels like progress. Selling is uncomfortable: you put yourself out there, people say no, you find out that what you built isn't exactly what someone wanted to pay for. But a product nobody knows about isn't a backup plan, it's an expensive hobby.

<Callout type="warning" title="What it cost">
  The backup plan wasn't free. It was more than a year of nights and weekends, and when I needed it,
  it still wasn't ready to carry me on its own. If you're thinking about building one, count on the
  main cost being time, and on paying for the time you don't put in today on the day you need it.

## What I take away

If I had to sum it up in one sentence: build the backup plan while you don't need it yet, and build it on top of something you're already doing. You don't need to quit. You don't need a genius idea. You need to start with what you have, bring in someone who knows what you don't, and go sell before you feel ready.

I didn't do it all right. I started in time, but not early enough. I built a lot and sold little. And still, when my contract ended, I wasn't back at zero. That was all I wanted.
